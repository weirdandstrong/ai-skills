# Question Bank

The actual questions. Ask them in plain language, one at a time in Phases 0–3.

These are written the way you should say them. Resist the urge to make them more professional — a coach answers a blunt question honestly and a polished question with marketing copy.

## How to ask

**One at a time in Phases 0–3.** Six questions at once gets you the three easy answers and silence on the one that mattered.

**Let them ramble, then reflect back the sharpest fragment.** Coaches think out loud. The usable material is almost never the summary they give at the end — it's the aside in the middle, the thing they said quickly because they assumed it was obvious. When you hear it, quote it back verbatim and ask if that's the real answer.

**Prefer their words to yours.** If a coach says "people show up already apologizing," never upgrade that to "clients present with anticipatory deference." You just deleted the brand.

**When an answer is abstract, ask for the last specific time it happened.** Abstraction is where the generic lives. A story has details you can build with.

## Phase 0 · Intake

### The history path — ask everyone, regardless of tenure

- How long have you been doing this, and how many people have you worked with — roughly?
- Tell me about one client or member you were unusually good for. What was going on with them when they arrived?
- Now tell me about one you couldn't help. What happened?
- When someone quits or doesn't renew, what reason do they give? And what do you think the real reason is?
- What do people say to you at the door, or in the first five minutes of a first call? Same words, over and over — what are they?
- If you have testimonials or reviews, paste them in. Don't clean them up. The typos are data.
- What do your staff or peers hear from clients that you don't?

### The failed attempts — spend real time here

Failures name the enemy faster than successes do, because they mark where you tried to be something the market refused.

- What have you tried in the last few years that didn't work? A program, a pivot, a marketing push, a positioning?
- Walk me through one of those. What did you hope would happen, and what actually happened?
- How did it feel to be doing that work? Not whether it made money — whether you wanted to keep doing it.
- Is there something you *used* to do that you quietly stopped, and never announced?

### The self path — ask everyone, including ten-year veterans

- What were you doing for a living before this?
- Were you ever the person you now work with? What happened to you?
- What do people ask you about at parties, once they find out what you do?
- What do you find yourself ranting about? To your partner, to a friend, in your head at 2am?
- What's the thing you believe about this work that would get you in trouble with your peers?

### The toolkit

- List every certification, training, and method you use with clients — all of them, in the order you actually reach for them in a session.
- Which one do you lead with when you describe what you do? Which ones do you use but never mention?
- Are there terms from those trainings you'd never want changed or reworded?

### The existing assets

- What's the business called right now, and where did that name come from?
- What are your programs or services called?
- Send me your website, your Instagram bio, your LinkedIn headline — whatever's public.
- On a scale of "carved in stone" to "burn it down," how attached are you to the current name?

*(Ask the attachment question regardless of the answer you expect. The Contradiction Check still runs either way — knowing where they stand just changes how you deliver it.)*

## Phase 1 · The Enemy

Open here. It works on almost everyone:

> **What's the lie in your industry?**

Then follow the heat. Below are prompts for each of the four categories if they stall.

### The industrial complex

- Who makes money when your clients stay stuck?
- What's an entire industry built on a problem never getting solved?
- What do people spend money on for years without getting better?
- Whose business model requires your client's problem to be permanent and complicated?

### The received wisdom

- What's the standard advice in your field that you think is wrong?
- What does every peer of yours say that makes you wince?
- What's true but useless? (Often more interesting than what's false.)
- If you gave a talk at your industry's conference and told the truth, what would make the room go quiet?

### The adjacent category

- What do people assume you are, that you're not?
- Who gets confused with you, that you want nothing to do with?
- Finish this: "People think this is ___, but it isn't."
- What's the compliment you hate receiving?

### The false cure

This one doubles as market research — a client's failed purchases are a map of the whole category.

- What has your client already bought that didn't fix this? List everything.
- What did they think that thing was going to do?
- Why didn't it work — mechanically, not emotionally?
- What do they say about it now? ("I did therapy for three years and—")

### Ranking and pressure-testing

- Of everything you just said, which one would you argue about at a dinner party, unprompted, with someone you'd just met?
- Now make the best case *for* the thing you're against. Seriously — the strongest honest version.
  - *(If they can't, they don't understand it well enough to attack it, and the audience will hear that. Work on this until they can.)*
- Is there anyone you're against that you're actually just jealous of? Worth knowing before it ends up in the copy.

### Then run the Inversion Pass

Give them: the obvious enemy, the genuine inverse, and a third framing that moves the problem into a different category. Ask which one makes them lean forward.

## Phase 2 · The Archetype

Elicit from history, not aspiration. The archetype has to be something they already are on a good day.

### Where they came from

- What did you do before this, and what did you get good at there?
- What skill from that life do you still use every day in this one?
- What did people rely on you for, before you were a coach?

### How they actually work

- Think of a session that went really well. What were you doing — with your hands, your voice, your body? Were you sitting? Moving? Writing?
- What do you do that other coaches in your space don't do?
- What do you flatly refuse to do that most of them do?
- When a client is upset, what's your instinct? Move toward it, name it, redirect it, sit in it?
- Are you more useful in the first ten minutes or the last ten?

### What people already call them

- What do people come to you for that isn't coaching?
- Has anyone ever described you in a way that stuck? What did they say?
- What's your nickname, if you have one? Who gave it to you?

### The extension test

Once you have candidates, run this out loud. It's the difference between an archetype and a decoration.

> From inside this metaphor, name:
> 1. The product
> 2. The first free thing someone gets
> 3. The verb on the button
> 4. What it's called when someone is stuck
> 5. What it's called when it works

If four of five come easily, it's a system — keep it. If you're straining, it's decoration — take the next candidate.

### The two hero journeys

- Tell me your version of this story: what were you like before you understood this, what happened, and what are you now?
- Now the same story for your client. Same enemy, different rank.

### Vocational-Fit Check

- Could you make this argument, in this voice, every week for a year — and still want to?
- Is this who you actually are on a good day, or who you'd like to be?
- Reading this back: does it make you want to go post something right now, or does it make you tired?

## Phase 3 · The Expensive Problem and the Transformation

**The stuck phase. Go slow. Do not accept the first answer, or the second.**

### The expensive problem

Open:

> **What expensive problem do you solve, and for exactly whom?**

Then the drill. Ask "and what does that cost them?" repeatedly until the answer contains a number, a relationship, or a span of years.

- What does this problem cost them in money? Be specific — a promotion, a season, a client roster, a business.
- What does it cost them in relationships? Who else is paying for this?
- How long have they had it? What did it cost them last year that they haven't counted?
- What's the thing they'd be doing right now if this weren't happening?
- What do they lose that they can't get back?

**Reject these answers:** stress, overwhelm, not living up to potential, lack of confidence, feeling stuck. They're symptoms, they're free, and everyone claims them. Keep going.

**Accept answers like:** a marriage, a body they can't get back after 50, a promotion that went to someone else, a business that stayed at $400K for six years, a decade in a career they chose at 22.

### The transformation

Open:

> **Describe what someone looks like before, and what they look like after — from the outside. What would a stranger watching notice?**

- What can they do after that they couldn't do before? A specific action.
- What do they stop doing?
- What's the first thing that changes — the earliest visible sign it's working?
- What does someone in their life say about them, three months in?
- If I filmed them before and after, what's in the two clips?

**If the change is purely internal, keep digging.** Internal shifts always have external tells, and the tell is the thing you can actually sell. "More confident" becomes "asks for the number without apologizing first." "Less anxious" becomes "eats lunch away from their desk." "More self-worth" becomes "left the gym at 6 instead of staying until 9."

### Cost of inaction

- If they do nothing and we talk in two years, where are they?
- What's the version of this that ends badly? How does it actually end?
- Is there a deadline built into their situation — an age, a contract, a body, a kid getting older, a business running out of runway?

### Price sanity

- What are you charging now, and how did you land on that?
- Against everything we just listed as the cost of inaction, is that number ridiculous in either direction?
- What would you have to believe about yourself to charge triple?
- Who else in the market charges what for what, and what do they actually deliver?

*(If the price is a small fraction of the cost of inaction and it still feels expensive to say out loud, the problem is the articulation of the cost, not the price. Fix the articulation.)*

### Gate A

Before moving on, run the Vocational-Fit Check again and a short adversarial pass:

- Say your positioning in one sentence, out loud, as if to a stranger at a party.
- Who would hear that and immediately not be your client? (If nobody, it's too broad.)
- What's the strongest argument that you're wrong about this problem?
- Is anyone already solving this well? What would they say about your version?

## Phase 4 · The People

Batching is fine from here on.

### Deriving the failure modes

- Think about everyone you've worked with on this problem. Does it show up the same way in all of them, or are there different flavors?
- Sort them: what are the two to four *characteristic* ways this goes wrong in different people?
- For each one, what's the sentence that person says to themselves? (Not what they say to you — what runs underneath.)

### Per persona

- Name them. Give me an age, a job, a life situation.
- What do they say out loud on a first call?
- What do they say to their partner at 11pm?
- What are they thinking in the parking lot before they walk in?
- What do they say when they cancel?
- **What have they already spent money on for this?** List everything — apps, programs, therapists, courses, equipment, retreats, supplements, another gym.
- Looking at that list: what kind of thing do they buy? Are they buying amplifiers, escape routes, vibes, systems, or permission? *(What they buy reveals what they think is wrong with them — which is usually the misdiagnosis you're correcting.)*
- What compliment do they not notice you paying them?
- What's the thing you could say that would make them feel seen and slightly caught?

### Buying readiness — ranked separately from pain

Ask explicitly, because these come apart and conflating them builds businesses around people who never purchase.

- Which of these people has actually paid you? Which ones almost did?
- Who has the money and the permission to spend it on themselves? (Those are two different things and the second one is usually the constraint.)
- Who reads everything you post for a year and never buys?
- Who, when they buy, brings other people with them?
- If you could only serve one for the next six months, which one — and is that the same as the one you find most interesting?

*(Building the voice for one persona while taking money from another is a legitimate strategy. It just has to be a decision rather than an accident.)*

### Objections

- What are the five things people say when they don't buy?
- For each: what's the belief underneath it? What is that objection defending?

*(Every objection is the persona's script defending itself. The response isn't to argue — it's to name it back.)*

### Grounding

Use web search to find how these people actually talk in public — forums, reviews, subreddits, comments under competitors' content, job posts if it's work-related. Bring back real sentences. Synthesis plus real language beats either alone.

**Then flag it:** these personas are hypotheses until real humans have been interviewed. Phase 6 assigns that.

## Phase 5 · The Voice

### The NO list

- What words in your industry make you cringe?
- What phrase, if it appeared on your website, would mean something had gone wrong?
- What do your *clients* say that you'd never repeat back to them? *(These are the tempting ones — a coach mirrors client language by reflex.)*
- What's the compliment that means you've drifted?

For each banned word, write one line on why. The reason is what makes the rule survive a contractor who doesn't share the coach's taste.

### The YES list

- What terms are yours — that you made up, or use in a specific way nobody else does?
- From your certifications and trainings: which terms do you keep exactly as taught, and why? *(Fidelity here is credibility. Don't let anyone rename these.)*
- What words from your audience's own world can you borrow to make this land instantly?

### Registers

- How do you sound when you're trying to stop a stranger from scrolling past?
- How do you sound on a sales call with someone who's nervous about the money?
- How do you sound in the room, with a client, when nobody's watching?

*(Same voice, three volumes. If they're three different people, that's a bait-and-switch and Phase 6 will catch it.)*

### Anti-patterns

- Send me three things you've written that you *almost* liked. Not the bad ones — the near-misses.

Near-misses teach far more than obvious failures, because the failure is subtle and nameable. For each: show the original, name the specific axis it failed on, rewrite it.

### The gut check

Build five yes/no questions any piece of copy has to pass, derived from what *this* brand keeps getting wrong. Not a template — a specific set of tripwires for their specific drift.

## Follow-up moves that work on any answer

- **"When was the last time that happened? Walk me through it."** Converts abstraction into a story with usable detail.
- **"Say that again, shorter."** Coaches over-explain. The third version is usually the line.
- **"You said [exact quote]. Is that the real answer?"** Reflecting a verbatim fragment is the single highest-yield move in the whole kit.
- **"What would you say if you weren't worried about sounding arrogant?"** Removes the hedge that's flattening the answer.
- **"Who would be annoyed by that?"** If nobody, it's too safe to be a position.
- **"What's the version of that you'd only say to a friend?"** That's the brand voice. The other one is the website voice they already have and don't like.
