# The Brand Bible Template

Phase 7. Assemble everything into one working document.

**Write it as a tool, not a trophy.** The test: could a contractor, a copywriter, a new staff member, or an AI tool be handed this document with no other context and produce something on-brand? Every section should be usable by someone who wasn't in the room.

**Keep the coach's own words wherever possible.** If the Bible reads better than anything the coach has ever said, you've overwritten them, and they will quietly stop using it. Their phrasing, sharpened — not your phrasing, imposed.

**Show, don't characterize.** "The voice is bold and direct" tells a writer nothing. Three example lines and three counter-examples tell them everything. Every voice claim in this document should be followed by evidence.

## Cover

Brand name. Version number (start at 1.0). Date. Who operates it. One line on what the document is for and who should read it.

Include one sentence naming what this document is the source of truth *for* — landing page copy, content, designer briefs, staff onboarding, AI tools. It tells a reader whether they're in the right place.

---

## PART I · The Foundation

**1.1 The Flag** — one paragraph: what this brand is against, and what it's the antidote to. Name the enemy explicitly. This paragraph is the reason the rest of the document is different from every other brand guide, so it goes first.

**1.2 Positioning statement** — one paragraph in this shape: *For [specific person] who [specific expensive situation], [brand] is [what it is]. Unlike [alternative], which [what it does wrong], we [what we do instead].*

**1.3 The operating verbs** *(if the archetype produced them)* — two or three verbs that name what the brand does, defined so they can't be used interchangeably. A brand with two well-defined verbs can describe its whole funnel in four words.

**1.4 The promise** — what a client will have at the end, stated concretely enough to be checked.

**1.5 Who this is for** — bullets, specific. Roles, life stage, situation, what they've already tried.

**1.6 Who this is not for** — bullets, blunter than feels comfortable. This section does more work than 1.5. It's what makes the right person feel found.

---

## PART II · The Persona

**2.1 The archetype** — who the coach is in this work. Written as a portrait, not a label. What they do, what they refuse to do, what makes them different from the obvious version of the same archetype.

**2.2 What we are / what we are not** — a two-column table of paired adjectives. Each pair should be a near-miss: the thing we are, beside the thing we're often mistaken for. *Mechanical / mystical. Direct / cruel. Warm / soft.* Near-misses calibrate; opposites don't.

**2.3 Operating principles** — four to seven postures, each with a heading and a short paragraph explaining *why* it holds. These are what someone reasons from when the document doesn't cover their situation, so explain the logic rather than just stating the rule.

**2.4 The two hero journeys** — the coach's, against the enemy. The client's, against the same enemy at a different rank. Short. These are the source of every origin story, About page, and podcast answer the coach will ever need.

---

## PART III · Voice

**3.1 The voice principles** — five to seven tests every line of copy passes. Each written as a question, each with an off-brand example and an on-brand example. The examples are the actual content; the principle is just the label.

**3.2–3.4 The registers** — one section per register. For each:

- Where it lives (specific surfaces — not "social media" but "LinkedIn openers, video hooks, Substack headlines")
- How it sounds (bullets)
- Its cadence (the beats it moves through — e.g. *interrupt → mechanism → next move*)
- Four to six sample lines at full strength
- Length conventions where they matter
- What it never does

Brick-and-mortar adds a fourth register: the floor voice.

---

## PART IV · Lexicon

**4.1 The YES list, in three layers.** Keep the layers visibly separate — they have different rules.

- *Layer 1 — Proprietary terms.* Table: term, when to use it. These are the brand's own words; use them with stubborn consistency, because repetition is what turns a coined term into an asset.
- *Layer 2 — Methodology vocabulary, verbatim.* Table: term, what it means, which training it comes from. **These are never renamed or spun.** The fidelity is the credibility line back to the coach's actual qualification, and renaming makes their own training illegible to everyone else in that world. A coach with three modalities has three sub-layers; all three stay intact.
- *Layer 3 — Bridge vocabulary.* Table: term, how we use it. The audience's own language, borrowed so the work lands instantly.

**4.2 The NO list.** Table: banned word or phrase, and one line on why. The reason is what makes the rule survive a contractor who doesn't share the coach's taste.

Include a line about client language: whether banned words stay banned when a client uses them. (Usually yes — mirroring is the most common way a voice erodes.)

If any word looks banned but isn't — because it's load-bearing in the methodology — say so explicitly with the exception spelled out. Ambiguity here produces either sloppy copy or over-correction.

**4.3 Concept glossary.** The brand's proper nouns — named concepts, characters, programs, artifacts. Two to eight, no more. A cast that needs a chart is a cast nobody will remember.

---

## PART V · Persona Hooks

One section per persona. For each:

- **The pain we name** — one line, in their language.
- **The flattery they don't notice** — what we respect about them that we never say directly. This is what makes copy land instead of insult.
- **The provocation** — the uncomfortable true thing only they need to hear.
- **A hero line** — one quotable sentence aimed at exactly this person.
- **Vocabulary that lands** — six to ten words from their world.
- **What they've already bought** — the failed-purchase list, which doubles as the objection map.
- **Buying readiness** — where they sit, and whether this brand is built to capture their attention, their money, or both.

Mark the beachhead persona clearly.

**If personas have not been validated against real interviews, label them UNVALIDATED at the top of Part V.** A coach who forgets which parts are hypotheses will build a year of content on someone who doesn't exist.

---

## PART VI · The Copy Engine

The part that makes the voice reproducible by someone who isn't the coach.

**6.1 Headline formulas** — four to six fill-in-the-blank patterns, each with two or three filled examples. Build them from *this* brand's enemy and archetype so the pattern already carries the position.

**6.2 Hook structures** — three to five opening patterns for the first seconds of anything.

**6.3 CTA patterns** — what the buttons and closers say. Include a short list of CTAs this brand never writes.

**6.4 The manifesto** — 600 to 1,000 words of the voice at full length, no headers, no bullets, the argument made in full.

This is the single most useful artifact in the document. It becomes the calibration reference: when anyone is unsure whether a draft fits, they read it back to back with the manifesto and listen for the difference. It does more to make a voice reproducible than any list of adjectives ever will. Write it in the coach's voice, from their material, and read it back to them before locking it.

---

## PART VII · Visual Identity

Stub at this stage:

> Visual system to be built in a separate session. See the **Production Kit** skill — colors, typography, logo standards, imagery direction, environment, and video grammar, all derived from the archetype and voice established in this document.
>
> Interim rule: [one or two constraints already implied by the archetype — e.g. "no stock photography," "no gradients," "mono type for anything diagnostic"]

Resist building the visual system now. Identity first, production second — visual work done before the voice is settled gets redone, and a coach who spends their energy on a palette before their positioning is sound has been badly served.

---

## PART VIII · The Quality Bar

**8.1 The gut check** — five yes/no questions any piece of copy must pass before it ships. Derived from what *this* brand keeps getting wrong, not from a template. If a coach's drift is toward softness, one question should catch softness.

**8.2 Anti-patterns** — four to six worked examples in this shape:

> **Failure — [name of the failure mode]**
> **Off:** [the near-miss copy]
> **Why:** [the specific axis it failed on]
> **Rewrite:** [the fixed version]

Use the coach's own near-misses. They teach far more than invented bad examples, because the failure is subtle and the coach recognizes their own reflex.

## Colophon

- Brand, operator, document version, date.
- Methodology sources, credited by name. *(If the coach is certified in someone else's method, name the method and its originators. It costs nothing and it's the honest thing.)*
- **Known contradictions** — anything the Contradiction Check surfaced that the coach chose to keep. Written as a decision, not an oversight.
- **Rejected cuts** — the stress-test charges the coach declined, with their reasoning. Useful later, when someone asks why.
- **Version history** — what changed between versions and why. This document is going to move, and the coach needs to see what moved.
- One closing line: the question to ask when in doubt. Usually a compressed restatement of the archetype. *"When in doubt, ask: would [the archetype] do this? If no, rewrite."*

---

## Delivery

Markdown as the source of truth. Offer .docx and .pdf as well — coaches share these with designers, contractors, and staff who won't open a markdown file.

Version it 1.0 and tell the coach plainly: this document changes. The stress test will produce revisions, the field test will reshape the personas, and the first three months of actually using it will teach them more than this session did.
