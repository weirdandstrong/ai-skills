# Worked Example

The case this kit was reverse-engineered from. A real brand, live, built in about two weeks of AI-assisted sessions.

Read it for calibration — how sharp the output should be, and what each phase actually produces. Show a coach the Phase 1 and Phase 2 outputs when they ask what they're going to end up with; it's more persuasive than any description of the process.

**The brand:** `>beRewritten_` — a 90-day language and nervous-system coaching program for burned-out senior tech operators. Transformation-brand lens. Founder was certified in a language-and-breath coaching methodology and had fifteen years in tech, including his own repeated burnout.

---

## Phase 0 · Intake

Raw material on hand: three levels of certification in one methodology, four recorded beta group sessions, fifteen years in the industry he'd be selling to, and his own history as the exact person he wanted to serve.

The intake finding that mattered most: **he was the former patient.** That's rare and it shortens Phase 3 dramatically. When a coach isn't the former patient, Phase 4 has to work much harder and the field test matters more.

---

## Phase 1 · The Enemy

Where a generic kit would have asked "what's your mission," this asked what the lie in the industry was. What came back was three antagonists, not one — and naming them as a set turned out to be more powerful than a single enemy, because it defined a whole region of the market as hostile territory.

> **The Trauma Industrial Complex** — insists every problem requires five years of archaeology before you're allowed to feel okay.
> **The Wellness Industrial Complex** — sells you breathwork apps for a problem that won't be fixed by shallower breathing.
> **The Productivity Industrial Complex** — whispers that your nervous-system collapse is a calendar issue.

And the mechanism that made it a position rather than a complaint:

> Each of those industries has a vested financial interest in your problem being permanent and complicated. Our entire model assumes the opposite.

**What to notice.** All three have a *business model*, which is what separates a real enemy from a vibe. "Hustle culture" would have failed here — nobody profits from it in a way you can point at. "An industry that bills monthly for a problem it never solves" is falsifiable, specific, and immediately generates copy.

**The inversion that produced it.** The consensus position in this market is *burnout is a wellness problem requiring rest and self-care*. The weak inversion is *burnout is not a wellness problem*. The strong inversion changed the category entirely: **burnout is a mechanical fault in language, and mechanical faults don't need a five-year study — they need a wrench.** That recategorization is the entire brand, and it came from an inversion pass, not from a brainstorm.

---

## Phase 2 · The Archetype

The archetype: **a mechanic.**

Its origin was a line written for an earlier product: *"When your tire falls off on the highway, you don't need a longitudinal study from PhDs to fix it — you need a wrench."*

Then the critical refinement, which took a second pass: a mechanic normally fixes the thing for you, and that would have made the brand another authority selling dependence — the very thing it was against. So the archetype got a constraint:

> This mechanic does not turn the wrench for you. He reads the diagnostic, finds the fault, and hands you the wrench.

That one constraint made the archetype defensible against *both* enemies simultaneously — against the trauma industry (which makes the client a permanent patient) and the productivity industry (which sells a system to follow). It also happened to be a precise description of what the underlying methodology actually is: a questions-based practice, not an advice-based one.

**The extension test, passed:**

| Slot | Answer |
|---|---|
| The product | The 90-Day Rewrite |
| The free thing | The Syntax Diagnostic |
| The CTA verb | Apply · Take the diagnostic |
| Stuck state | A corrupted script |
| Working state | Patched · Compiled · Rewritten |
| The method's name | Syntax Audit → Patch the Code → Install the Defense |
| The diagnostic | The Cognitive Load Audit *(same thing, renamed for engineers)* |

Seven of seven, cleanly. That's what a real archetype looks like: one choice, and the entire naming problem is solved permanently. The logo fell out of it too — a terminal prompt and a blinking cursor.

**Calibration note.** If a coach's archetype can't fill five of these slots without straining, it's decoration. Throw it out and take the next candidate. Do not proceed with a pleasant metaphor that doesn't generate.

---

## Phase 3 · The Expensive Problem and the Transformation

**The expensive problem, in the brand's own words:**

> The burned-out tech operator who has tried everything reasonable and is one Slack ping away from torching the career they spent fifteen years building.

Note what makes it expensive: fifteen years of career capital, quantified elsewhere as *"somewhere between $400K of unvested equity and a career-ending breakdown."* Not "stress." Not "overwhelm." A number and a decade.

**The transformation, made observable:**

> In 90 days, you will know exactly which of your scripts is corrupted, exactly what to say to the people you've been avoiding, and exactly how to make a decision without your nervous system making it for you.

And the anti-promise directly after it: *"Not enlightenment. Not balance."* Naming what you're *not* promising is a credibility move most brands skip, and it costs nothing.

**Calibration note.** The middle clause — *exactly what to say to the people you've been avoiding* — is the observable one. A stranger could verify it. That's the standard to hold coaches to when they offer "feel more confident."

---

## Phase 4 · The People

Four personas, framed as **"four ways the same operating system fails"** rather than as demographics — Hero, Cynic, Hollow, Fawn. Each got a name, a script, a language bank, and a provocation.

The part most brand kits never do, and the reason to insist on it:

**Buying readiness was ranked separately from pain level.**

> Tier 1 — the Fawn and the Hollow buy now. Both have cash and both already consider coaching a category they purchase from.
> Tier 2 — the Hero is the flywheel. Most money, most pain, hardest ego defense. He will not buy "coaching." He will buy *a diagnostic engineering audit on himself.* When he buys, he refers other Heroes.
> Tier 3 — the Cynic consumes content for eighteen months before he buys. Build content for him. Do not bank revenue against him.
>
> **Strategic call: build the voice for the Hero and the Cynic — the voice is the moat. Monetize the Fawn and the Hollow in the meantime.**

That last line is a decision no brand kit would have produced from a persona template. It only appears when you force the two rankings apart.

**The spend pattern as diagnosis** — each persona's failed purchases were sorted by *category of thing bought*:

> The Hero buys **amplifiers**, never brakes. The Cynic buys **escape routes**. The Hollow buys **vibes**. The Fawn buys **systems**.
>
> None of them have paid for syntax. That is the gap I sit in.

Four words — amplifiers, escape routes, vibes, systems — and the entire market gap is visible. Push coaches for this level of compression.

**Objections as diagnosis:**

> Every objection is a syntax error speaking in its own voice. *"I don't have time"* → **I am important because I am needed.** *"Too expensive"* → **I am not worth this investment.** *"I should be able to fix this myself"* → **Asking for help means I'm broken.**
>
> The objection isn't a problem to overcome. It's the syntax error showing up for the diagnostic. The right response isn't to argue. It's to name it back.

## Phase 5 · The Voice

**The NO list came from the enemy, and it's the reason the voice is enforceable.** Twenty banned words, each with a reason: *healing journey, holding space, self-care, wellness, authentic self, manifesting, container, lean in, just breathe.* Plus the rule that makes it hold: **banned even when a client says them. Especially then.**

One entry is worth studying because it's the kind of nuance a template never catches. The word *thoughts* looks like it belongs on a banned list in this brand. It doesn't — it's load-bearing in the methodology (*observe your thoughts, think about your thinking*). So the Bible bans exactly one form of it: `"Thoughts?"` as a post closer, with a paragraph explaining why the rest is fair game. Precision like that is what stops a NO list from producing over-corrected copy.

**The YES list, in three layers:** proprietary brand terms (Override, Rewrite, Script, the Competence Trap) · methodology vocabulary kept verbatim from the certification, unrenamed and credited · tech-operator bridge vocabulary (bus factor, single point of failure, latency, root cause, race condition).

The middle layer is the one coaches get wrong. The temptation is to rename a certification's terms so they fit the metaphor. Renaming severs the credibility line back to the training and makes the coach illegible to everyone else in that world.

**Three registers, mapped to funnel stages:** Combative (content — the loudest, and the one everything else follows from), Diagnostic (landing pages and sales — same voice, tools set down), Coaching-Room (in-program — warmer, no audience, still mechanical).

**Voice principles as tests with paired examples** — not adjectives:

> **Did we name the trap, or just describe a feeling?**
> Off: *"You're feeling overwhelmed."*
> On: *"You're catching everyone else's chaos and calling it leadership."*
>
> **Did we hand them a wrench, or just hand them a hug?**
> Off: *"It's okay to feel that way."*
> On: *"Open a doc. Write 'What I am actually pretending not to know.' Go."*

## Phase 6 · The Stress Test

Run about a month after the Bible was locked, and it produced **nine hard cuts, all of which were accepted.** Including:

- Differentiation was aesthetic, not categorical
- The marketing voice and the delivery voice didn't match
- Four personas was three too many for a beachhead
- The price needed proof
- Safety and scope exposure was the non-negotiable
- The brand-building itself had become a burnout script — for a founder whose brand is about burnout
- Day-job collision
- Premature automation

That's a lot of damage to a document everybody had been proud of a month earlier. It also produced the decisions that made the business real: one beachhead persona instead of four, a lower founding price, and a written screening-and-referral protocol.

**This is the argument for Phase 6.** Every one of those nine was findable at the time the Bible was written. Nobody looked, because the document was exciting and internally coherent. Coherence is not correctness, and an AI that helped build something will not spontaneously attack it.

---

## Phase 7 · Assembly

A versioned Brand Bible — v2.1.1 by the time it settled, with a changelog documenting every shift and why. Roughly 15,000 words across eight parts, delivered as markdown with .docx and .pdf exports.

It has been handed to AI tools, used to generate a website, a hundred-post content catalog, an application form, and email sequences — all of which came out recognizably in the same voice, months later, without the founder in the loop for each one.

**That is the actual test of a Brand Bible.** Not whether it reads well. Whether someone else — a contractor, a staff member, a model — can be handed it and produce something that sounds right.

---

## The two transferable lessons

**One archetype did all the naming.** Product, diagnostic, phases, CTA verbs, logo, visual system, even the failure states — all generated from a single metaphor choice. If a coach finds themselves inventing each of those separately, the archetype is too weak. Go back to Phase 2 and take another candidate.

**The banned list is what made the voice survive.** Anyone can write one good paragraph in a brand voice. What makes a voice hold up six months later, in the hands of someone else, is a specific list of words that get a draft rejected — and a rule that the list applies even when a client uses those words first.
