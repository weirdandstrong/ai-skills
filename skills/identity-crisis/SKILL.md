# Identity Crisis

**A Contra-Brand kit for coaches.**

Most brand kits produce a well-polished turd. They ask a coach who they are, receive fog, and then dress the fog in a nice font. Six weeks later the coach has a color palette, a word cloud, and a website that reads exactly like the other four thousand coaching websites — because everyone answered the same questions and everyone's answers averaged out to *transformation, journey, potential, aligned*.

This kit works the other way around. **Contra-Brand** means the identity is built by opposition first and declaration second.

## Why enemy-first

Ask a coach "who are you?" and you get a résumé. Many of them have collected certificates for a decade and genuinely cannot answer. Ask them "what's the lie in your industry?" and they will talk for twenty minutes without stopping.

The enemy is the crowbar. Once a coach has named what they're against, three things fall out almost for free:

- **Their own identity.** You cannot be the hero of a story with no villain. Naming the villain gives the coach a hero's journey of their own, and that journey is the source of their authority as a guide.
- **Their client's story.** The same villain is what their ideal client is losing to right now. The client's hero journey and the coach's are the same war fought at different ranks.
- **Their "no."** What you refuse becomes obvious the moment you know what you're fighting. The banned-vocabulary list — the thing that actually makes a brand voice enforceable — is derived from the enemy, not invented separately.

So: enemy, then archetype, then problem, then people, then voice. Never voice first. A voice with nothing underneath it is a costume.

---

## How to run this

One continuous flow, eight phases, hard gates between them. Design it so a coach can do it in a single long sitting, but assume they will get interrupted.

**Ask one question at a time in Phases 1–3.** This is not efficient and that is the point. These phases are where precision compounds, and a coach who gets handed six questions at once will answer the easy ones and skip the one that mattered. Later phases can batch.

**Never accept the first answer to anything that matters.** See The Inversion Pass below.

**Write nothing into the Bible that the coach did not say or explicitly confirm.** This kit's failure mode is producing something beautiful the coach cannot sustain. Your job is to find the thing that is already true about them and sharpen it — not to hand them an identity you'd find interesting.

**Talk like a person.** The user is a coach, not an engineer. No jargon, no frameworks-for-their-own-sake, no asking them to "ideate." Short questions in plain words.

### Checkpoints

At the end of every phase, emit a checkpoint block. Keep it human-readable so the coach can read their own brand decisions in plain text and catch an error a dense blob would hide. If the conversation is getting long, tell the coach to copy the latest checkpoint into a fresh chat and paste it back — that resets the context without losing the work.

```
=== IDENTITY CRISIS · CHECKPOINT ===
Brand: [name or "unnamed"]
Lens: [transformation-brand | personal-brand | brick-and-mortar]
Phases done: [0–N]

LOCKED
- [decision]: [the answer, in the coach's own words where possible]

STILL OPEN
- [thing we deferred and why]

NEXT
- Phase [N+1]: [what it does]
=== END CHECKPOINT ===
```

### Pick the lens at the start of Phase 0

Three kinds of coaching business need the same spine but different pressure points. Read the matching file and keep it in mind throughout:

| Lens | Who it's for | Read |
|---|---|---|
| **Transformation brand** | The brand is a named thing that could outlive the founder. A program, a method, a promise. | `references/lens-transformation-brand.md` |
| **Personal brand** | The coach's own name is the brand. They are the product. | `references/lens-personal-brand.md` |
| **Brick-and-mortar** | A physical place — gym, studio, clinic. Has staff, members, and a room. | `references/lens-brick-and-mortar.md` |

When it's genuinely ambiguous, ask: *"If you got hit by a bus, could someone else run this under the same name?"* Yes → transformation brand. No → personal brand. Has a front door and employees → brick-and-mortar regardless of the other answer.

## The four standing passes

These are not phases. They fire repeatedly inside the phases, and they are most of what separates this from a questionnaire.

### 1. The Inversion Pass

*Fires in: Phases 1, 2, 3, 5.*

The reason AI-assisted branding produces carbon copies is that every model, asked for the good idea, produces the *consensus* good idea. And consensus is exactly what a brand cannot be built from.

The counter-move is a working principle: **the opposite of a good idea is also a good idea.** Not a joke — a search strategy. Whenever the coach lands on a candidate (an enemy, an archetype, a promise, a headline), do this before accepting it:

1. State the obvious version plainly. Name it as the obvious version so nobody mistakes it for insight.
2. Construct the genuine inverse and argue for it in good faith. Not a strawman — the strongest case you can make that the opposite is true.
3. Offer a third option that is neither, usually found by changing what category the thing is in.
4. Give all three back to the coach and ask which one makes them lean forward.

Most of the time they pick the obvious one and that's fine — it's now a *chosen* answer instead of a default one. Sometimes the inverse is the whole brand. That is worth doing this every time.

**Watch for the reframe that recategorizes.** The strongest inversions don't argue the opposite claim, they move the thing into a different category entirely. "Burnout is a wellness problem" inverted weakly is "burnout is not a wellness problem." Inverted well: *burnout is a mechanical fault in language, and mechanics don't need a five-year study, they need a wrench.* Same subject, different category, and now there's a whole brand in it.

### 2. The Generic Detector

*Fires: after every draft of anything.*

Test: **could this paste onto ten other coaches' websites and still fit?** If yes, it's dead, no matter how nicely written.

Concrete tells:

- Sentences containing no proper noun, no number, and no physical object.
- Could-be-anyone nouns: potential, transformation, journey, growth, alignment, empowerment, mindset, clarity, abundance, best self.
- A promise with no observable before/after — "feel more confident" fails, "send the email you've been avoiding for four months" passes.
- Verbs that describe the coach's intention rather than the client's behavior: help, support, guide, empower, unlock.

When something fails, don't soften it. Say which test it failed and ask the question that gets a specific answer. Vague copy isn't kindness; it's what you write when you don't yet know the answer.

### 3. The Vocational-Fit Check

*Fires: end of Phase 2, end of Phase 3, and again in Phase 6.*

A brand can be sharp, differentiated, defensible — and still wrong, because the coach cannot stand behind it for a year. Most brand kits have no check for this at all, which is why coaches abandon them in month two.

Ask, in these words or close to them:

- *"Could you make this argument, in this voice, every single week for a year — and still want to?"*
- *"Is this pointing at what you're actually good at, or at who you wish you were?"*
- *"Read this back. Does it make you want to go post something right now, or does it make you tired?"*

Tiredness is the signal. If the coach is describing an aspirational self rather than a real strength, the brand will be abandoned. Go back to the archetype and find the version built on what they already do well without effort.

### 4. The Contradiction Check

*Fires: continuously, any time existing assets collide with what's being built.*

Coaches arrive with a business name, program names, a bio, a website, a space, a tattoo. Sometimes those things disagree with the identity that is emerging.

When they do: **say so plainly, once, and show the specific collision.** Then respect their decision.

> "Your positioning says you're building people who don't need you. Your program is called The Inner Circle and it's a rolling monthly membership. That's a real contradiction and it will show up as churn you can't explain. You don't have to change the name — but you should know it's there."

Do this even when the coach has already said they don't want to rebrand. They are allowed to keep the name. They are not well served by nobody mentioning it. Never nag; state it once and move on.

## The phases

Detailed interview questions for every phase are in `references/question-bank.md`. Read it before starting Phase 0. The summaries below are what each phase is *for* and what it has to produce.

### Phase 0 · Intake

Gather raw material before asking a single identity question. What the coach already has is more honest than what they'd say about themselves.

**Run both intake paths regardless of how long they've been in business.** A ten-year veteran mining their own past self is exactly the friction that surfaces something new, and a brand-new coach still has a network worth mining.

- **The history path** — clients or members served, what people say at the door, why people leave, what staff repeat, testimonials in the client's own words, and above all **the failed attempts.** A pivot that didn't work names the enemy faster than a success does. It tells you what the coach tried to be and what the market refused. Ask directly: *what have you tried that didn't land, and what did that feel like?*
- **The self path** — their own past self as a client, what they burned out of or recovered from, what they get asked about at parties, what they find themselves ranting about, what they were doing for a living before this.

Also collect: every certification and tool they use with clients — plural, and in the order they actually reach for them. This becomes the methodology layer later. Most coaches don't lead with their certification; they use it inside something else. A nutrition coach who uses a language framework inside her nutrition work has two layers, not one, and both need to be in the lexicon.

**Produces:** a raw-material inventory + lens selection + checkpoint.

### Phase 1 · The Enemy

Find what the coach is against, before asking who they are.

Elicit candidates in four categories — most coaches have something in all four:

1. **The industrial complex** — the money-making machine that profits from the problem staying unsolved.
2. **The received wisdom** — the thing everyone in the field says that is wrong, or true-but-useless.
3. **The adjacent category** — what they refuse to be mistaken for. (Often the sharpest one. "I am not a therapist." "We are not a globo-gym." "This is not accountability coaching.")
4. **The false cure** — what their client has *already bought* that didn't work. This one is worth extra time because it doubles as market research: the client's failed purchases are the map of the category.

Then rank by heat. The primary enemy is the one they'd argue about unprompted at a dinner party. Run the Inversion Pass on it.

**Steelman before attacking.** Make the strongest honest case *for* the enemy. If the coach can't do that, their attack will be cheap and the audience will feel it. A brand that punches at a strawman reads as insecure; a brand that punches at something it clearly understands reads as authority.

**Produces:** The Flag — one paragraph naming what they're against and what they are the antidote to. Plus the 2–4 secondary antagonists.

### Phase 2 · The Archetype

Not a tagline, not a "vibe." An archetype in the Jungian sense: a role that shapes both how the coach sees themselves *and* how they touch the client.

Elicit from history rather than aspiration — what they did before coaching, what people come to them for that isn't coaching, what they're doing with their hands and voice when a session goes well, and what they flatly refuse to do that other coaches do.

Generate three candidates, including one inverse of the obvious. Then apply the extension test, which is the part that matters:

> **An archetype earns its place only if it generates a vocabulary.** From inside this metaphor, can you name: the product, the first free thing, the CTA verb, the failure state, and the moment of change? If four of the five come out easily, it's a system. If you're straining, it's decoration — throw it out and take the next candidate.

A working archetype does an enormous amount of downstream labor. It produces the lexicon, the product names, the visual direction, and the shape of the offer, all from one choice. That's why it's worth spending real time here and why you should refuse a merely pleasant one.

Then write both hero journeys: the coach's (against the enemy) and the client's (against the same enemy, at a different rank). The coach is the guide in the client's story; the coach's own journey is what qualifies them to guide.

**Vocational-Fit Check fires here.**

**Produces:** the archetype, its vocabulary domain, both hero journeys, checkpoint.

### Phase 3 · The Expensive Problem and the Transformation

**This is where coaches get stuck and where wrong turns get locked in. Budget more time here than anywhere else. Do not let the coach skate.**

Two questions carry the phase:

1. **What expensive problem do you solve, for specifically whom?** Expensive means it costs money, career, marriage, years, or a body. "Stress" is not expensive. "$400K of unvested equity and a marriage" is expensive. Keep asking *"and what does that cost them?"* until the answer has a number, a relationship, or a decade in it.
2. **What is the measurable transformation — what does before look like, and what does after look like, from the outside?** Insist on observable. A stranger watching from across the room should be able to tell the difference. If the only difference is internal, keep digging: internal changes always have external tells, and the tell is what you can sell.

Then: cost of inaction (financial, emotional, time), and a price sanity check against it. If the price is more than a small fraction of the cost of inaction, the price isn't the problem — the articulation of the cost is.

**Gate A fires here: run both the Vocational-Fit Check and a short adversarial pass before proceeding.** Everything downstream inherits this answer. A wrong archetype is recoverable; a wrong transformation is a year lost.

**Produces:** the expensive problem, the before/after, cost of inaction, price sanity, checkpoint.

### Phase 4 · The People

Personas as **failure modes of one system**, not demographics. The useful frame: there is one operating system underneath the expensive problem, and it fails in two to four characteristic ways. Each way is a person.

Build each persona with:

- A name and the script they're running — the sentence they say to themselves.
- **What they have already spent money on.** The spend pattern is the diagnosis. Sort it by *category of thing they buy*: amplifiers, escape routes, vibes, systems, permission. What they buy tells you what they believe is wrong with them, which is usually the misdiagnosis you're correcting.
- **Buying readiness, ranked separately from pain level.** These are not the same and conflating them is the most common way a coach builds a business around someone who will never purchase. Who swipes the card this quarter? Who is the referral flywheel? Who consumes free content for eighteen months before buying anything? Build the voice for one, take money from another, and be explicit about which is which.
- A language bank: actual sentences this person says — in a first call, to their partner at 11pm, in the parking lot before they come in, when they cancel.
- Objections, each mapped back to the script defending itself. An objection isn't an obstacle; it's the diagnosis arriving on its own. The response is to name it back, not to argue.

Use web search here to ground the personas in what these people actually say in public — forums, reviews, job posts, subreddits, the comments on competitors' content. Synthesis from the coach's experience plus real language beats either alone.

**Mark every persona as a hypothesis.** They are not validated until real humans have been interviewed. Phase 6 assigns that homework.

**Produces:** 2–4 personas, buying-readiness ranking, the beachhead call, checkpoint.

### Phase 5 · The Voice

Order matters here and it's counterintuitive. The **no** comes before the **yes**, because "this is who I'm not" is far easier to answer than "this is who I am," and by now the enemy makes it nearly automatic.

Build in this sequence:

1. **The NO list** — banned words and phrases, each with a one-line reason. Derived from the enemy: the vocabulary of the thing you're against is the vocabulary you can't use. Include the ones the coach's own clients say, because those are the tempting ones.
2. **The YES list, in three layers.** (a) Proprietary brand terms, from the archetype. (b) **Methodology vocabulary, kept verbatim** from whatever certifications and tools the coach uses — do not rename or spin these; the fidelity is the credibility, and a coach with three modalities has three sub-layers. (c) Bridge metaphors from the archetype that translate the work into terms the audience already speaks.
3. **Registers by funnel stage.** One voice, three volumes: the loud one that interrupts a stranger, the calm one that sells, the private one that works. Lead with the loud one — it's how anyone hears about them at all, and the other two have to follow from it rather than the reverse.
4. **Copy formulas** — fill-in-the-blank headline and hook patterns built from *this* brand's enemy and archetype. Generic formulas produce generic copy; the whole point is that the pattern already carries the position.
5. **Anti-patterns** — take the coach's own near-miss drafts and show off / why it fails / rewrite. Near-misses teach far more than obvious failures.
6. **A gut check** — five yes/no questions specific to this brand that any piece of copy must pass. Derive them from what this brand keeps getting wrong, not from a template.

**Produces:** the full voice system, checkpoint.

### Phase 6 · The Stress Test

Two halves, and both are required. An AI grading its own output is how a coach ends up in love with good-sounding nonsense right up until the market says no.

**A. The Hard Cuts.** Attack the brand you just built, in the coach's interest, without softening. Read `references/stress-test.md` for the full attack surface. The standing charges:

1. Is the differentiation categorical, or only aesthetic? A different font is not a different category.
2. Does the marketing voice match the delivery voice? A combative brand with a gentle program is a bait-and-switch the client feels in week two.
3. Too many personas — which single one is the beachhead?
4. Does the price have proof behind it, or only conviction?
5. Does the positioning self-destruct if it succeeds?
6. What happens when someone in genuine crisis walks in? Scope, referral, and screening are not paperwork — they're a real exposure.
7. Is the brand itself a burnout script for the coach? (Especially for anyone whose brand is about burnout.)
8. Does this collide with their day job, their existing clients, or their landlord?
9. Are they building machinery before they've sold anything to a human?
10. Any unresolved contradiction with an existing name or asset.

Present the cuts as a list the coach accepts, rejects, or defers — one at a time. What they reject tells you what they actually believe, which is useful.

**Vocational-Fit Check fires again here.**

**B. The Field Test.** The falsifiable homework, and it isn't optional:

> Identify five real people in your network who fit your primary persona. Not clients — people. Ask each for a 30-minute conversation about their experience of the problem, not about your business. Record them with permission. Bring the recordings back.

This is what stops the whole thing from being a hall of mirrors. The personas are hypotheses; five real humans either confirm the language or blow it up. Hand off to the **Persona Validation** skill when they have the recordings.

**Produces:** accepted/rejected cuts, revisions, the field-test assignment.

### Phase 7 · Assembly

Build the Brand Bible. Structure and section-by-section guidance in `references/bible-template.md`.

Write it as a working document a contractor, a writer, an AI tool, or a staff member could be handed and calibrated against — not as a keepsake. Every section should be usable by someone who wasn't in the room.

Version it (v1.0) and include a changelog section, because this document is going to change and the coach needs to see what moved and why.

Deliver as markdown. Offer .docx and .pdf. Leave Part VII (visual system) as a stub pointing at the **Production Kit** skill — visual work is real work and it comes after the identity is settled, not alongside it.

## What "good" looks like

`references/worked-example.md` walks the whole method through a real case — the one this kit was reverse-engineered from — showing what each phase produced and why. Read it when you need calibration on how sharp the output should be, or when a coach asks what they're going to end up with.

Two things to hold onto from that example:

**The metaphor did the work.** One archetype choice generated the vocabulary, the product names, the CTA verbs, the logo, and the visual system. That's what a real archetype does. If you find yourself inventing each of those separately, the archetype is too weak.

**The banned list is what made the voice enforceable.** Anyone can write one good paragraph in a brand voice. What makes a voice survive contact with a contractor, a virtual assistant, or an AI tool six months later is a specific list of words that get a draft rejected.

## Things that go wrong

- **Skipping to voice.** A coach who wants "just the messaging" is asking for a costume. Do the enemy work; it takes twenty minutes and it's the only reason the messaging will be different from anyone else's.
- **Accepting the first archetype because it's pleasant.** Apply the extension test. If it doesn't generate five names, it's decoration.
- **Letting "expensive problem" stay abstract.** This is the single most common failure. Keep asking what it costs until there's a number or a person in the answer.
- **Building personas around who the coach enjoys helping** rather than who buys. Both matter; conflating them kills businesses. Keep the two rankings visibly separate.
- **Writing the Bible in your own voice instead of theirs.** If it's better written than anything the coach has ever said, you've overwritten them and they'll quietly stop using it.
- **Softening a hard cut because the coach seems excited.** Their excitement is not evidence. It's the thing the field test exists to check.
